## Structured source capture

### Skills acquisition versus skills application

The article opens with a two-stage distinction attributed to early behaviour therapy:

1. Skills Acquisition
2. Skills Application

Robertson argues that people can accumulate meditation, visualization, journaling, affirmation, or other self-help skills while remaining anxious because they do not apply those skills to real situations. Avoidance—including subtle forms such as procrastination—interrupts transfer from practice to life.

### Compartmentalizing skills

A skill can appear effective in a protected practice context but disappear under actual pressure. Journaling or meditation may therefore support growth, or may become another way to delay confronting feared situations. The diagnostic question is not whether a person practises regularly, but whether the practice changes behaviour when discomfort appears.

### Stoic mental rehearsal and modern imaginal exposure

The article connects Stoic *premeditatio malorum* (“premeditation of adversity”) and Posidonius’s *proendēmein* (“inhabiting in advance”) with modern mental rehearsal. Its modern reference point is imaginal exposure: deliberately bringing a feared but manageable situation to mind, accepting the resulting discomfort, and remaining with it rather than escaping or trying to eliminate anxiety.

The intended attitude is “relaxing into anxiety” rather than trying to “relax it away.” Starting with manageable stress and gradually increasing difficulty is described through the analogy of stress inoculation.

## Activity: imaginal exposure

### Safety first

- Choose only a mildly uncomfortable and manageable situation—not the worst fear or a traumatic experience.
- People experiencing panic attacks, dissociation, trauma-related symptoms, self-harm urges, or severe or overwhelming distress should not confront difficult material through this exercise without a qualified mental-health professional.
- Stop and return attention to the present surroundings if the exercise becomes overwhelming, unsafe, or disorienting.

### Preserved 25-step sequence

1. Settle into the method.
2. Close the eyes and attend to natural breathing without deliberately changing it.
3. Repeat a chosen centring word or phrase on each out-breath.
4. Continue for roughly one minute.
5. Bring a mildly anxiety-provoking situation to mind.
6. Choose something normally worried about or avoided that produces only mild anxiety.
7. Imagine that it is happening now; perfect visualization is unnecessary.
8. Notice sights, sounds, bodily feelings, stress, and discomfort.
9. Continue the centring phrase on the out-breath.
10. Keep returning attention to breathing while imagining the situation.
11. Repeat the chosen word or phrase slowly on each exhalation.
12. Use it to remember that the feelings need not be removed and can run their natural course.
13. Relax into the anxiety rather than trying to relax it away.
14. Notice automatic reactions such as tension, faster heartbeat, stomach sensations, warmth, or anxious thoughts.
15. Do not suppress or control those reactions.
16. Notice urges to struggle—holding the breath, tensing, or wishing the feelings away.
17. Release the struggle as far as possible and remain with the imagined situation.
18. Stay with the scene a little longer.
19. Continue imagining while repeating the centring phrase.
20. Treat either an increase or decrease in anxiety as acceptable; neither is the exercise goal.
21. Practise staying instead of avoiding and discover that the feelings are tolerable.
22. Return attention to the present.
23. After at least five minutes, or up to ten, let the scene fade.
24. Open the eyes slowly and attend to the surrounding room.
25. Notice changes in anxiety intensity, attitude toward anxiety, and confidence in coping.

## Claimed mechanism

The author frames anxiety as involving both an appraisal of how bad a situation is and an appraisal of one’s ability to cope. Imaginally approaching the situation challenges “I cannot cope” or “I cannot stand these feelings” and is intended to develop what Albert Ellis called high frustration or discomfort tolerance.

The article also claims that emotions naturally fade over time and that giving up avoidance allows discomfort to run its course. This is the author’s explanatory framing; the source does not compare alternative contemporary models of exposure learning or provide clinical outcome evidence.

## Source questions worth preserving

- Is a self-regulation practice changing real behaviour, or merely postponing contact with discomfort?
- Can a skill be rehearsed under a small, safe dose of the context in which it must eventually work?
- Does the exercise increase willingness and perceived coping ability even when anxiety does not decrease during the session?
